Should You Buy a Flat, or Land?

Before you decide, look beyond the brochure — consider the long-term costs, maintenance, debt, flexibility, resale demand, and the value of what you are building for the future.

Something we've heard since childhood

However little a person earns, they should have a roof over their head — a home of their own. That much is true. A safe address of one's own really does matter in life.

But in city and small-town life, "a home of one's own" now often just means a flat. A gleaming building, a lift, security — a picture of modern living is put in front of us. But the real question is: the flat that looks so beautiful today, what will it look like in 20 years? That's the question we rarely ask.

A new flat's biggest appeal — its newness Flat

When a new flat is first built, it looks stunning — fresh paint, a new lift, modern design. But time doesn't stand still. After 10, 15, 20 years the building ages, and construction-quality issues can surface — damp walls, peeling paint, maintenance problems that creep up slowly.

And as a flat owner, you can't simply tear down the whole building and rebuild it as you please — your ceiling is shared with someone else's floor, your wall is part of another flat's wall, and the neighbours above and below have rights too.

The flat may be yours, but you don't have sole control over the whole building.

A 20-year loan — a big question

Say you buy a flat and take out a 20-year loan. At first it feels like: "I'm paying my instalments, and one day the flat will be fully mine." But by the time that day comes, the flat itself is nearly 20 years old too. In other words, by the time you're debt-free, the property is no longer as new as it once was.

The question that follows — how attractive will that property still be in the market, after all those years of payments?

Demand for an older flat can fall

New generations are usually drawn more to new designs and modern living. What feels modern today can feel dated in 20 years — and by then, newer projects will have come up around it. As demand for the new rises, the pull of the old can fade.

So this reasoning alone isn't enough — "whatever price I paid today will surely double in 20 years." There's no guarantee that a property's price simply rises with time. Local development, demand, construction quality, age and market conditions all matter.

Supply versus demand

An asset whose supply can be expanded easily doesn't always see unlimited price growth. Building after building can go up in an area — where one housing project stands today, several more can rise right beside it in a few years. In other words, the supply of flats keeps growing.

Land is different. The world's total supply of land is not unlimited. Population keeps growing, settlements keep growing, and the need for roads, markets and jobs keeps growing — so scarcity of well-located land is a meaningful factor.

What makes land special? Land

A house can grow old after 20 years. If you want, you can tear it down and rebuild. But the land stays. Today there might be an old house on it; tomorrow that house can be torn down for a new one — the house ages, but the plot itself does not.

This is exactly why land can matter so much for building wealth across generations. That doesn't mean buying land anywhere is a guaranteed win, though — location, road access, legal paperwork, future development and usability all still matter.

The amenities in the brochure aren't always the ones you get

Big-city advertisements talk about swimming pools and clubhouses, but in a small town or mofussil district like Nadia, what most new flats actually offer is a much shorter, much more ordinary list:

A lift (not always running)
Generator backup — limited power
One security guard / gate
One open or covered parking spot
A rooftop water tank, own borewell
Shared access to the roof

These do make daily life easier, no question. But the question stays the same — how much will you actually use each one, and don't you still pay maintenance on the parts you barely use?

In other words, buying a flat isn't just about the instalment — loan payment + maintenance + other costs = your real monthly outgo. Many people don't work out this full number properly.

Asset, or liability?

This is the most important lesson in the whole picture. An asset that puts money into your pocket behaves like a true asset. Something that regularly takes money out of your pocket behaves like a liability, no matter what it's called.

In smaller towns, rental rates are often low relative to the property price — so the gap between the instalment and the rent can stay wide. That gap is what can make a flat behave like a liability in cash-flow terms, for as long as the loan runs. On the other hand, if the rent you'd earn comes close to or exceeds the instalment and other costs, the property's financial position can look far stronger. So it's not enough to simply see "I own a flat" — the real question is what that flat is actually doing for you.

A flat to live in and a flat as an investment aren't the same thing

Buying to live in yourself brings real value — security, family, schools, hospitals, commute, community. If you plan to stay there for the next 15–20 years, and the instalment sits comfortably within your means, there's a genuine case for buying.

But buying it as an investment is a different calculation entirely — what rent can you realistically expect, what's the instalment, what's the maintenance, what will demand look like down the line, how old will the property be, how much new supply is coming into the area, and how easy will resale be. Assuming "the price will go up" is not enough on its own.

"I'm buying it for my children" — worth thinking through too

Many parents say, "I'm not buying this for myself — I'm buying it for my child's future." It's a lovely thought. But the real question is: will that child even want that same old flat in 20 years? By then, their preferences and needs may have changed completely.

An old flat isn't easy to tear down and rebuild either, because it's part of a shared building. If it's land instead, the next generation gets the freedom to build whatever fits their own needs, whenever they need it.

Teach Children to Value What They Have

There is another side of buying a home for our children that is easy to overlook: teaching them to value what someone worked hard to build.

If a child grows up understanding how many years of work, saving, sacrifice and patience went into building a house or buying a flat, they are more likely to see that home as something worth protecting and caring for.

Without that understanding, a child may grow up seeing a house, furniture, doors or other possessions simply as things that can be replaced whenever they are damaged.

If you understand the effort behind something, you learn to respect the thing itself.

Anger can make a person damage what took years to build. A door can be repaired, but the habit of treating other people's hard work without respect is much harder to repair.

So giving children a home is only part of the responsibility. Teaching them gratitude, self-control, responsibility and respect for property is an equally important inheritance.

Don't just leave your children a house. Leave them the values that will help them take care of it.

Not about appearances — about financial strength

A colleague bought a flat, a friend bought a new car, someone you know lives in a big housing complex — and suddenly it feels like, "I should have something like that too." But we never see how much debt sits behind it. The shine is visible; the loan behind it isn't.

The goal isn't to look impressive — it's to be financially strong.

So should you never buy a flat?

No, it isn't that simple. It wouldn't be right to say flats are a bad idea. It's more accurate to say: "Don't buy a flat without understanding why."

When a flat can be a good decision

  • You genuinely need a place to live
  • Security matters to you
  • You want to live in a good location
  • You can comfortably manage the instalment
  • You plan to stay long-term

When it's worth rethinking

  • Buying purely to impress others
  • Taking on more debt than you can handle
  • Buying only because "the price will go up"
  • Not accounting for maintenance costs
  • Not looking at the gap between rent and instalment

For a middle-class family, a home of one's own is never just property — it's a dream, a sense of security, and one of life's bigger milestones. But if reaching that dream means 20–25 years of instalments, maintenance, and financial strain, it's worth pausing to think it through.

The biggest lesson for the middle class

A home of your own genuinely matters. But in the pursuit of it, make sure you don't lose your financial independence along the way.

If a flat fits your real needs, your sense of security, and your means — it can be a very good decision.

Last word

A home isn't just bricks and mortar — it means security, family, peace of mind. But buying property calls for arithmetic alongside the emotion.

Before you decide, drawn in by a new flat's gleaming lobby, ask — what will it look like in 20 years? Alongside the instalment, ask — what will maintenance cost? Alongside the hope of a rising price, ask — will there still be demand? And most importantly — am I buying an asset, or a liability I'll be paying for, year after year?

A flat isn't a bad choice for everyone. But before buying, be clear about this: are you buying somewhere to build a life, or looking at it as an investment? The two call for very different arithmetic.

Value financial independence over appearances. Build assets that put money into your pocket in the future — not ones that keep taking it out.
