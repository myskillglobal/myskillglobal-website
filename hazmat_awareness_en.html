Road Safety Awareness

What to do when you see a hazardous chemical vehicle on the road

Every day, tankers and trucks carrying flammable, toxic, or otherwise hazardous chemicals travel on public roads. Learning to recognise these vehicles, keeping a safe distance, and warning others are simple habits that can help prevent a serious accident.

Please note — This page is an informational effort created for general public awareness. It is not an official government guideline. For anything safety-critical, always follow the instructions of local authorities and traffic police.
☣
Know the symbols

Hazard symbols you'll see on these vehicles

The colourful diamond-shaped symbols fixed on a vehicle's body tell you what kind of chemical is inside. Learning to recognise them makes it much easier to judge the risk from a distance:

Explosive

Can explode from shock, heat, or friction

Flammable

Catches fire very easily

Toxic

Harmful if inhaled, touched, or swallowed

Corrosive

Damages metal or skin on contact

Oxidizer

Intensifies fire and burning

Gas Cylinder

Carrying gas under pressure

🔍
How to identify

How to recognise a hazardous chemical vehicle

These vehicles are designed to be recognisable from a distance, so you can be alert well before one gets close. If you see any one or more of the following markings, the vehicle is very likely carrying hazardous cargo, not ordinary goods:

✓A large diamond-shaped hazard symbol on the vehicle body
✓Reflective, high-visibility stripes running around the vehicle
✓Bright yellow or orange warning panels
✓A board displaying the vehicle's hazard class and UN number
✓A scannable QR-coded emergency information placard
✓A flashing amber beacon on the roof or cabin
✓A distinct siren, sounded when needed
✓Front and rear cameras may also be fitted

Even if such a vehicle is stopped after an accident, its warning lights should keep flashing — so approaching traffic can still recognise it from a distance.

↔
Safe distance

How much distance to keep

A tanker carrying chemicals behaves differently from an ordinary vehicle — it needs more room to stop, more space to turn, and can suddenly lose balance because of the liquid load shifting inside.

🚚
Minimum 100–150 metres
🚗

Following from behind

Keep a much larger gap than usual, so you have room to react safely if it brakes suddenly.

While overtaking

Avoid overtaking such vehicles near bridges, flyovers, or sharp bends.

At signals or in traffic

Try not to stay parked directly beside or right behind one for long at a signal or in a jam.

After an accident

If the tanker ahead is involved in an accident, stop your vehicle at a safe distance and wait — do not go closer to help. Wait for emergency responders.

📢
Warning others

Help make others aware too

Staying alert yourself isn't enough — part of this awareness is helping the people around you stay safe as well:

Signal to others

Use your headlights or horn to let the driver behind you know there's a hazardous vehicle ahead.

Talk to people around you

Point out these markings to family, neighbours, or classmates so they can recognise them on their own too.

Watch for local signage

Pay attention to roadside boards that read "Hazardous chemical vehicle in transit" or "Maintain safe distance."

Extra care near schools and hospitals

If you spot such a vehicle near a school or hospital, keep children and patients well away from it.

⚠
In an emergency

What to do if you see a leak or accident

✔ Do

  • Move away to a safe distance immediately — at least 100 metres or more.
  • Move upwind (the direction the wind is coming from), so smoke or fumes don't drift toward you.
  • Call your local emergency number or the police right away — share the location and any details from the vehicle's placard, if visible.
  • Tell other pedestrians and drivers nearby to avoid the site.

✘ Don't

  • Don't approach the vehicle yourself to try to help.
  • Avoid getting close to a spill or fumes to take photos or videos.
  • Don't smoke or light a flame near the site, especially with flammable cargo.
  • Don't try to cross the road near the site until emergency responders arrive.
🕒
Timing & routes

When these vehicles are usually on the road

In many places, vehicles carrying flammable or hazardous chemicals try to avoid busy school, college, and hospital hours (roughly 7 AM to 10 PM), moving instead late at night or before dawn on designated routes. Keep this in mind:

Extra alertness at night

Stay especially alert to these vehicles when driving near highways or industrial areas at night or in the early hours.

In bad weather

Movement of these vehicles is generally meant to reduce during heavy rain, fog, or storms — but if you do see one, keep an even greater distance, since braking takes longer in these conditions.
