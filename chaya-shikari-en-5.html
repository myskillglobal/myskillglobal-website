Fifth Chapter: The One-Crore Offer

Since childhood Amar had loved thinking about machines. Even under his new identity in Debu’s house, his old dream had not died. Whenever he had spare time he sat in a small workshop and carried out experiments. He had begun working on an idea to make fuel from kitchen waste, agricultural residue and other organic material. At first the experiments failed. Sometimes the mixture clotted, sometimes the temperature would not hold. Still, he did not give up.

One day a local shopkeeper saw a small machine he had made and showed interest. A few farmers also asked whether this technology could make use of their waste. Amar understood that his idea had real value. He kept his designs, tests and cost calculations in order.

Around this time a foreign industrialist, Richard Hamilton, came to the area on a business trip. Hearing of Amar’s project through a local acquaintance, he visited the workshop. Amar was frightened at first. But Richard listened patiently to his explanations, examined samples and asked about the potential of the technology.

“Your work is still at the development stage,” said Richard, “but the idea has market value. If the tests, the safety checks and the terms of the contract are satisfied, we can consider buying the rights.” After several rounds of discussion, technical review and ownership checks, an offer of one crore rupees—ten million—arrived. Amar could hardly believe it—the man who had once wanted to end his own life had now earned such a price with the work of his mind.

On the night before the contract was to be signed, a single hurricane lamp burned on the table in the workshop. Amar was bent over the design of the machine, but his eyes were not on the paper. There was a soft sound at the door behind him. Arati came in with a cup of tea in one hand, and something hidden under the end of her sari in the other.

“Working so late?” “Not working—afraid,” said Amar with a smile. “Tomorrow is a contract for a crore. And yet I feel that somewhere something is not right.” Arati set down the cup. “So you feel it too.” Crickets sang outside. For a while neither spoke. Then Arati said in a low voice, telling him of the night she had stood outside her father’s door and heard the words ‘the last consignment’ and ‘choosing people’.

“I love my father, Amar. But is love a permission to keep one’s eyes shut?” From under her sari she took out an old black-and-white photograph. Her father stood with some foreigners at a dock. On the back were the stamp of a foreign studio and a name written by hand—Sheikh Abdullah. “I found it as a child in an old box of his. I never dared to ask.”

Amar looked at the photograph. Arati looked away and said, “When Arun left, he said he would write within two months. Two letters came. Then nothing. All this time I thought he had forgotten me. Lately I think he perhaps couldn’t write.” Her voice did not break; only her fingers tightened on the cup. Amar said nothing. He only moved to one side of the bench to make room, as though pain could be shared a little.

After a long time Amar said, “Richard sahib travels in many countries, knows many people. If I am wrong, the photograph will tell him nothing. But if I am right…” Arati looked at him. “If you are right, you will go against the man you call your life-giver.” Amar slowly shook his head. “I can’t go against the people on that list, Arati.” Arati placed the photograph in his hand. Their fingers touched for a moment; neither drew back.

The next day the signing of the contract was completed, and a crore rupees did come into Amar’s hands. When Debu stepped outside to take a phone call, Amar, with a trembling hand, laid the photograph before Richard and said in a low voice, “Have you ever seen this man?” Richard looked at the picture for a long time and said nothing. Later, comparing his own documents, he found the same face in an international investigation list, under another name—Sheikh Abdullah. The record mentioned inquiries in several countries on charges of human trafficking and illegal organ trade.

Returning, Debu laid a smiling hand on Amar’s shoulder. “It isn’t right to carry so much money about, Amar. Leave it with me; it will be safe.” Amar paused slightly. “I want to send it home first, Babu. I haven’t given them any news for so long.” For a moment Debu’s smile froze. It stayed on his lips, but not in his eyes. He only said, “Good. Do as you think best.” Amar did not understand then how much calculation lay behind that silence.

Richard accused no one before he was sure. He asked the relevant authorities to verify the information. When news reached him, Debu was shaken. The identity he had built over many years might collapse. He began planning to sell his property, move documents out of reach, and flee abroad with Arati.

That night Debu sat alone in his study for a long time. He unlocked a drawer and took out an old photograph—a woman’s face, a large vermilion mark on her forehead. He gazed at it for a long while, then turned it face down and shut the drawer. No one in the house had ever seen that picture.

Amar now knew what he had done. The hand that had once pulled him from the mouth of death—he himself had taken the first step against it. It was not the joy of victory but a strange emptiness that closed around him. He could only remember the touch of Arati’s fingers that night in the workshop—which had given him courage even in the midst of fear.
