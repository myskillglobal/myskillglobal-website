Introduction

Big Gains, Fast — The Danger of Financial Shortcuts

Many of us grow up with a strange mindset: get more, faster.

Fast success in school, fast progress at work, fast profit in business, and in investing — "double your money in no time!" This craving for speed often pushes people toward shortcuts. But as in most areas of life, shortcuts in finance can come at a very high price.

⚠️ The Dream of Quick Profit

When someone sees "$10,000 today, $12,000 tomorrow!" it looks like the easy path. But they often forget to ask an important question:

"What if that $10,000 becomes $8,000 instead?"

However attractive the upside looks, the downside deserves the same attention. In financial markets, higher potential return usually comes with higher risk.

📉 Trading, Intraday, and Options — Why Caution Matters

Trading, intraday dealing, and derivatives such as futures and options are not the same as ordinary long-term investing. They try to profit from short-term price movement. In particular, leverage and derivatives can let a small amount of capital control a much larger exposure. If the market moves the wrong way, losses can grow just as quickly.

Short-term trading in derivatives is widely considered a specialist activity, not a beginner-friendly way to build wealth — it demands active monitoring, deep product knowledge, and a tolerance for fast, sizeable losses that most long-term investors don't need to take on.

Treating derivatives trading as an easy income stream is dangerous.

Building wealth follows the same pattern: savings → regular investing → good assets → reinvestment → time → compounding. There is no magic here, and no shortcut.

💰 Growing Little by Little

Suppose someone invests a modest, affordable amount every month. In the first few years, the results may not look impressive at all. But if they —

  • invest regularly
  • choose assets thoughtfully, not impulsively
  • avoid frequent, unnecessary buying and selling
  • reinvest a portion of the income they receive
  • look for opportunity during market corrections
  • and stay invested over a long period

then, gradually, a real asset base can take shape.

🏦 Cash Matters Too

There's no need to invest every last dollar at once. Markets sometimes get expensive, and sudden corrections can appear without warning. Keeping some cash on hand makes it easier to act when a genuine opportunity shows up.

Cash isn't just money sitting idle. Often — cash is future opportunity.

⏳ The Biggest Shortcut — There Isn't One

He once said —

"If you ask me the safest way to get rich quickly, I'd say: stop trying to get rich quickly."

"Give wealth-building time. Understand the risk. Invest regularly. Reinvest. Keep some cash for opportunity. And let the market have the time it needs."

🌳 A Closing Thought

Today's world tells us — "move fast." But when it comes to building wealth, a better rule might be —

"Go slow, but keep going for a long time."

The lure of quick profit can lead people into risks whose damage takes years to undo. Wealth built slowly, on the other hand, may look unexciting at first — but it tends to grow stronger with time.

A long-term plan for durable wealth matters far more than the dream of quick profit. In financial life, the goal isn't finding a shortcut — it's the right path, the right level of risk, consistency, and patience.

🌍 Four Gardens on Four Continents

A story about cash flow, reinvestment, and compounding.

A father once told his son — "Earning money isn't enough on its own. At some point in life, you need to build assets that can also earn money for you."

His son asked — "Isn't that just buying shares?"

The father smiled and said —

Shares, REITs, InvITs, funds, trusts — each has its own structure. But there's one thing to understand: none of these create money by magic. Cash flow comes from the real assets or businesses behind them. A portion of that cash flow reaches investors, under various rules.

💡 Where Does the Money Actually Come From?

This is the first thing to understand. If an investment distributes a large sum every year, somewhere behind it, the underlying assets or business had to generate that real economic cash flow.

Real Asset / Business
↓
Revenue
↓
Operating Expenses
↓
Interest / Tax / Other Obligations
↓
Distributable Cash Flow
↓
Investor Distribution

🏢 The Story of a REIT

Suppose a REIT owns office buildings, commercial property, or warehouses. Various companies and businesses rent that space.

Property
→
Rent
→
Revenue
→
Less Expenses
→
Distributable Cash Flow

A portion of this cash flow may be distributed to unit holders. In other words, what an investor receives from a REIT ultimately traces back to real property and its rental income.

🛣️ The Story of an InvIT

Now consider an infrastructure investment trust. Its underlying assets might be various infrastructure projects — a road generating toll or contractual income, a power-transmission line earning transmission charges, a pipeline earning usage-based income, or a renewable-energy asset earning income from power sales.

Infrastructure Asset
↓
Revenue from Users/Customers
↓
Operating Cost
↓
Interest / Tax / Other Obligations
↓
Distributable Cash Flow
↓
Distribution to Investors
So understanding an InvIT's distribution means more than looking at a distribution percentage — it means understanding the asset and cash flow behind that distribution.

🏭 What About an Ordinary Stock?

The core story for an ordinary company isn't very different. A company sells products or services: sales → revenue → profit/cash flow. The company can then choose to expand the business, reduce debt, build reserves, or pay shareholders a dividend.

So a company paying a dividend today is no guarantee the same dividend continues in future. If the company's business performance changes, the dividend can change too.

🏦 What About a Fund or Trust?

A fund or trust can hold many kinds of assets. Those assets can generate interest, dividends, rent, and other income. Depending on applicable expenses, obligations, and tax treatment, a portion is then distributed to investors.

So a fund paying you money doesn't mean the fund is paying out of its own pocket — it's the assets inside the fund creating that economic value.

💰 So How Is a 10% or 12% Yield Possible?

Suppose an income-generating asset is valued at 1,000 units of currency, and it produces a distributable cash flow of 100 units per year.

Asset Value1,000
Annual Distributable Cash Flow100
Approximate Cash Yield≈ 10%
A 10% yield does not mean a guaranteed 10% return.

Market prices can move. If the asset's market value falls to 800 while the cash distribution stays the same, the yield percentage rises. If the market value rises to 1,250, the yield on that same 100 of cash flow falls.

Yield and total return are not the same thing.

📈 Does Receiving a Distribution Mean a 10% Gain?

Suppose you invest 100,000. Over the year you receive a distribution of 10,000, but by that point the market value of your investment has fallen to 95,000. Looking only at the 10,000 distribution, you can't conclude you made a 10% gain.

Investment100,000
Distribution Received10,000
Market Value Now95,000

Because total return also depends on the change in market value:

Income + Capital Appreciation/Loss − Tax/Costs = Overall Result

🧾 And What About Tax and Withholding?

Suppose a distribution has a gross amount of 10,000. Under applicable tax rules, 1,000 is withheld, so 9,000 lands in your account.

Gross Distribution10,000
Tax Withheld1,000
Net Cash Received9,000

This withheld amount may show up as a credit on your tax records and can be adjusted or refunded when you file your final return, depending on your total tax liability. Cross-border investments can add another layer of withholding tax in the source country.

Before celebrating the gross figure, understand what the post-tax income actually is.

🔄 Then Compounding Begins

Suppose 100,000 produces 10,000 of income. If that income isn't spent but is put back into the investment instead, the investment base grows.

100,000
↓
10,000 Income
↓
Reinvestment
↓
More Units
↓
Future Income

The following year, those extra units can generate income of their own. Then reinvestment happens again. Kept up over a long stretch of time, this cycle produces the compounding effect.

The magic of compounding rarely shows up in a single year. The longer it runs, the bigger its effect becomes.

📈 What Did He Do When Prices Ran High?

One stretch, the market was doing very well. Prices across many assets had risen fast. Friends kept saying, "Put all your money in now!"

A good asset isn't automatically a good investment at any price.

So he invested only a little at a time. He never poured all his cash into the market at once — because he knew that today's high valuation could well be followed by a better entry point tomorrow.

📉 And When a Correction Came?

Some time later, the market corrected. Prices fell. Many people panicked. He, instead, looked at his cash reserve — but he didn't buy simply because prices had dropped. First, he checked:

  • What does the underlying asset actually look like?
  • Is the cash flow sustainable?
  • How much debt is involved?
  • Is the valuation reasonable?
  • Where does the distribution actually come from?
  • What caused the correction?
  • Are the long-term business fundamentals still intact?

Only once he was satisfied on all counts did he deploy cash, gradually.

I won't buy just because the price fell. I'll buy when a good asset is available at a reasonable price.

🏦 Why Does Cash Matter So Much?

His son once asked — "What's the point of keeping cash instead of investing all of it?"

Cash isn't money that simply earns nothing. Cash is an opportunity fund.

When the market runs expensive, cash gives you the ability to wait. When a sharp correction hits, that same cash can be put to work. Plenty of people spot good opportunities — but at that exact moment, they have no spare cash, and the opportunity passes them by. That was a mistake he refused to make.

⏳ The Real Test — Patience

Years went by. Sometimes the market rose, sometimes it fell. Sometimes distributions were generous, sometimes modest. Sometimes other investments outperformed his. He still didn't change his core plan, because he wasn't there to trade — he was building a long-term, income-producing portfolio.

Too much greed is harmful. So is too much fear.

🏠 What We Build, We Learn to Value

One day, his son asked him — "Dad, why do you care so much about the house, the garden, and everything you have built?"

The father smiled and replied — "Because when you understand what something costs to build, you learn to respect it."

A house is not just walls, doors, windows, and furniture. Behind it are years of work, saving, planning, sacrifice, and patience.

When a child grows up understanding that effort, the house becomes more than a place to live. It becomes something to protect and care for.

If we teach children only how to demand things, they may learn to treat possessions as disposable. But if we teach them the value of effort and responsibility, they begin to understand that breaking something in anger can destroy the result of someone else's hard work.

A door can be repaired. A broken habit of respect is much harder to repair.

The real lesson is not simply to protect a house.

It is to develop a mind that says:

"If someone worked hard to build it, I will respect it. If it belongs to me, I will take care of it. And when I build something myself, I will understand its true value."

That is another kind of wealth — the wealth of responsibility.

🌳 Ten Years Later

A decade later, his portfolio had grown substantially. He hadn't built it with one large sum on one big day — he built it through small, repeated investments. Layered on top of that were:

Distribution → Reinvestment → More Units
High Valuation → Slow Buying
Correction → Selective Buying
Cash Reserve → Opportunity
Time → Compounding

And above all — ⏳ patience.

🌱 Five Themes From His Approach

Starting Small
He invested regularly, within what he could afford.
Reinvesting Part of the Income
He treated today's cash flow as tomorrow's income generator.
Slowing Down When Prices Ran High
He never chased yield alone.
Keeping a Cash Reserve
He accepted that nobody knows exactly when the next opportunity will arrive.
Letting Time Do the Work
For him, time + reinvestment + discipline were the foundation of compounding.

🌍 The Final Scene

One evening, he was sitting in his garden. His son came over and asked —

"Dad, what have you actually built, all these years?"

He smiled gently and said —

"A garden."

The son looked around and said, "Where?"

"My investment portfolio — that's my garden."

"First, I planted money. Then income came from that asset. I reinvested part of that income. When the market got expensive, I waited. When corrections came, I used my cash to find new opportunities. And most of all — I gave the trees time to grow."

Wealth isn't built only by how much you invest. How long you stayed invested, how much you reinvested, how much patience you kept, and how ready you were when opportunity came — all of that matters just as much.
🌱 Today, a small seed.
🔄 Tomorrow, reinvestment.
💰 Then, cash flow.
📉 A correction brings new opportunity.
🏦 A cash reserve keeps you ready.
⏳ And, many years later — the fruit of compounding.

That, in short, was his philosophy:
invest slowly, understand where income comes from,
look at net cash flow, not gross,
weigh the tax and the risk,
keep some cash for opportunity —
and above all, be patient. 🌳
