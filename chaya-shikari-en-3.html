Third Chapter: The Search for a Hundred People

Within a few months Debu’s business began to decline. It was no longer easy to find people. Many village youths preferred to stay with small jobs; others were afraid to leave their families for an unknown country. Yet Debu’s plan required a large group. A ship, he used to say, did not pay for itself with fewer than a hundred passengers.

One day Debu called Amar and said, “You are also supposed to go abroad. But before that we need to gather more people.” Amar protested, “I thought I would learn work here.” Debu answered in a cold voice, “Your lodging, your food, your clothes—everything has cost money. If we count it up, you owe me. You can repay it with interest once you work abroad.” Alongside gratitude, the weight of debt now settled on Amar’s shoulders.

Debu began lending sums between ten thousand and fifty thousand rupees to helpless families. On paper it was help, in words it was an advance, but in truth it was a trap to hold people in place. Amar was told that he would only write down names, ages and papers. Before departure, everyone would be kept at a guesthouse outside the city.

The first day Amar was sent to a village with one of Debu’s men, he thought he was only going to check papers. Walking along a muddy path, he saw people sitting on the mud verandas waiting for them as though some government officer had arrived. One brought out old papers knotted in a gamchha; another told his son, “Bow to the Babu.” Amar felt uneasy. He told himself that these people were asking for work; he was forcing no one. Still, on the way back it struck him that he was calculating the price of hope from house to house—and he did not know in whose name the ledger of that calculation was really being written.

A Sister’s Wedding

Haripada worked as a day labourer. His father’s illness, his brothers’ schooling and his sister’s wedding had all landed on him at once. When he heard of good earnings abroad, he hesitated at first. Amar told him, “If you struggle for a few years, things at home will change.” In the end Haripada agreed.

The Fight for the Land

Nitai Mondal’s land had been mortgaged because of debt. He knew that if it was lost, his family would never stand on their feet again. The promise of earning abroad and sending money home seemed to him a last hope.

A Mother’s Treatment

The earnings of Gopal’s little tea stall could barely buy his mother’s medicine. Dues had piled up at the chemist’s. He thought that a few months of work abroad would clear all his debts.

The Weight of Shame

Madhu, unable to find work, felt like a failure before his family. His father’s look of despair burned in his chest. He saw the offer of a job abroad as a chance to win back his self-respect.

Skilled Hands

The evening light had faded. In a corner of the courtyard Malati sat at her old sewing machine, joining the sleeve of Lalitadi’s blouse. Apart from the steady rattle of the wheel, there was no sound. She had bought the machine with her husband’s first earnings; even now it seemed to carry the marks of his fingers. Two rupees a garment. Even sitting all day, the sums of rice and lentils never balanced.

Just then a woman came and stood there, a pot of sweets in her hand and a sweet smile on her face. She turned Malati’s stitching over, ran a finger along it and said, “I haven’t seen such fine stitches even in the big cities.” Malati lowered her head in embarrassment. Dropping her voice, the woman said that a big garment factory abroad needed skilled hands—two hundred and fifty rupees a month, food and lodging free. “Hands like yours are hard to find.”

“But I have never been outside the village,” Malati said slowly. “And my machine?” The woman laughed. “There the machines are new, run by electricity. You don’t even press with your foot.” Malati stayed silent for a long time. Then she got up and drew the old cover carefully over the machine, the way one draws a blanket over a sleeping child. “So when do I have to go?” she asked. She did not know whether she would ever hear that rattle again beneath the cover.

A Mother of Two

Kalpana had two small children. Her husband had left the family long ago. Promised work as a nursing assistant or domestic helper, she agreed to leave her children with a relative. Before leaving she laid her hand on her daughter’s head and said, “When I return, you will never struggle again.”

The Dream of a Hotel Job

Renu came from a poor family. When she heard about work in a hotel, she imagined placing her first month’s wages in her mother’s hands. Her hand trembled as she signed the papers for going abroad, but the agent’s assurances buried her fear.

The Arithmetic of a Pension

Nirmal Babu, a retired man, found it hard to manage medicines and household expenses on his pension. When he was offered a job as a security guard abroad, he thought that, old as he was, his experience still had value.

Mortgaged Land

Harimohan, an elderly farmer, had his land mortgaged. “If I can get the land back,” he used to say, “I can die in peace.” The promise of light work and good pay abroad persuaded him.

A Widow’s Cooking

Savitri Devi was a skilled cook. She was told that an old-age home abroad needed someone to cook. She thought that, alone as she was, she might at least spend the last years of her life earning her bread with dignity.

On the day of going to the guesthouse, a covered truck arrived at dawn at the edge of the city. Everyone gathered with bundles in their hands. Haripada’s sister pressed a jar of pickle into his hand and said, “Brother, send me a sari with your first pay.” Nitai kept looking back towards his land. Kalpana wiped her eyes and tried to smile in front of everyone. Amar counted heads and checked the ledger—sixty. A tick beside every name. Sleep in some eyes, a bundle on some shoulders, a trembling smile on some lips. The sound of the truck’s rear flap closing kept ringing in his ears for a long time.

And so, name by name, the names in Amar’s ledger multiplied. Every dream was different, but the weak spot in each of them was the same—poverty. Amar closed the ledger and thought that he might be giving so many people a chance at a new life. He did not know that the very pages of that ledger would one day become evidence of a crime.
