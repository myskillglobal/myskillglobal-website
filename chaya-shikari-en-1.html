First Chapter: The Afternoon on the Railway Tracks

The year was 1975–76. The monsoon had ended, and clouds still drifted across the autumn sky. At a small railway station the afternoon light was slowly fading. A kettle boiled at the tea stall on the platform, porters unloaded luggage, and far away the black smoke of a coal engine curled into the sky. The station clock showed half past five. Office clerks on their way home, villagers returning from the market and schoolchildren stood waiting for the train.

At one end of the platform stood a young man of twenty-six or twenty-seven. His name was Sanatan. There were shadows under his eyes, several days of stubble on his chin, and his shirt was dull with dust. In his hand was an old cloth bag, and inside it a shirt or two, some papers and an insurance receipt. A few rupees in his pocket—yet his mind was crowded with duties: his father’s treatment, his brothers’ schooling, his sister’s wedding, the debts of the household.

Once Sanatan had been a brilliant student. He had a strange pull towards mathematics and machines. The teachers at his village school used to say the boy would one day become an engineer. But his father’s illness, the poverty at home and the cost of studying stopped his dream at the college gate. He had done odd jobs—writing accounts in a shop, working as a temporary hand in a factory—yet nothing paid regularly. When he could not send money home, his mother’s letters were full of worry, and his father’s silence made him feel even guiltier.

He had taken out a life insurance policy worth fifty thousand rupees. Seeing his family’s name on the paper, he had first thought that at least in times of trouble they would have some protection. But long despair had swallowed his judgement. A dangerous calculation had crept into his helpless, confused mind—if he were gone, at least his family would receive some money. He had begun to measure the worth of a life in the figures of a policy. How wrong, how cruel that thought was—he no longer had the strength left to see it.

A train whistle sounded. The platform stirred suddenly. The tea-seller hurriedly took away the cups, a porter set down his load, a few passengers stepped back behind the marked line. Sanatan looked around once. He remembered his mother’s face, his little brother asking for his examination fee, the little jewellery his mother had saved for his sister’s wedding. For a moment his feet faltered. Then, as if shutting off every sound inside him, he walked slowly towards the track.

At that very moment a gentleman standing at the far end of the platform saw the scene. A costly kurta, polished shoes, a leather bag in his hand—his name was Debu. He had come to the station to meet someone. At first he did not understand what the young man was about to do. But as the noise of the train grew and he saw Sanatan’s unnatural advance, he ran.

In those days the points at that part of the station were worked by hand, with iron levers. Reaching the lever, Debu tried to shout a warning to the station staff. In the hurry he pulled the lever and changed the direction of the line. The train, instead of its scheduled track, swung onto the line beside the next platform. Passengers screamed and an uproar broke out across the station. Several workers came running. Sanatan was knocked to the ground, but he did not go under the train. He was alive.

Debu took him out of the platform. Sanatan’s body was trembling and he could not speak. In a quiet spot Debu asked, “Why did you try to end your life like this?” At first Sanatan stayed silent, tears gathering in his eyes. After a long time he said in a broken voice, “I can’t go on. Everyone is looking at me, but I have nothing in my hands. They all think I will do something. I can’t show my face to anyone.”

Debu made him sit and bought him water. “However big the problem, one cannot end one’s life before looking for a way out,” he said. Sanatan smiled bitterly and said he had tried everything. No work had come, debts had grown, and his father’s medicines were about to stop. If he received the insurance money, his family could at least manage for a while—that was why he had made this decision.

After some silence Debu said, “It isn’t even certain that your family would get the money. But it is certain that they would lose you. Did you mean to leave without speaking to them even once?” The words struck deep into Sanatan’s chest. He covered his face and wept. Debu comforted him and said he would deliver fifty thousand rupees to his family and give him a chance to stand on his feet again.

Sanatan looked at him with doubt. “You don’t know me. Why would you do so much for me?” Debu smiled gently. “Not every relationship needs a past. Sometimes one human being has to stand beside another.” Hearing this, a small trust stirred in Sanatan. But he could not read the calculating steadiness in Debu’s eyes.

“Your name is Sanatan—but from today you will live as Amar,” said Debu. “I have some work. You are clever; you can handle papers. Keep your old identity hidden for a while. Begin a new life.” Sanatan hesitated. Was it right to go without telling his family? Debu said he should first recover, and arrangements could be made afterwards. Once more he promised that the fifty thousand would be delivered.

The evening light had almost gone. One by one the station lamps came on. A few passengers farther off were still discussing the accident. Sanatan looked back once at the railway line. A few hours earlier all the doors of the world had seemed closed to him; now it seemed that at least one door had opened.

He got into Debu’s car. The car left the station and moved towards the dark road. Fields, trees and little shops fell behind the window. Sanatan did not know whether the money would truly reach his family, what the new work would be, or how long he would have to hide his old identity. Before he closed his tired eyes he thought only—perhaps now everything will be all right. The train vanished in the distance, but the strangest journey of his life had only begun.
