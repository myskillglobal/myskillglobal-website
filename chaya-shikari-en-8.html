Final Chapter: Sanatan’s New Sunrise

After the investigation, Sanatan’s true identity was verified and restored. All this time he had been known as Amar; now he got back his own name—Sanatan. Holding the document that bore it, his eyes filled with tears. He remembered his mother’s voice, the courtyard of his childhood, his brothers’ and sister’s faces, and the days when he had believed his life was over. With part of the money from the one-crore project, he cleared his family’s old debts—his sister’s wedding, his brothers’ schooling and the urgent expenses of the house.

Several agents of the criminal network were arrested. The safety of those who had been about to be sent abroad was secured. People like Haripada, Nitai, Gopal and Madhu were helped back to their families. But not everyone who had gone abroad earlier could be traced. Arun’s name remained in the case files—like an unfinished question.

When Kalpana returned to her two children, the little girl did not recognise her mother at first. Then she heard her mother’s voice, ran to her and threw her arms around her. Later it emerged that the picture-letter Arati had posted had indeed reached them; the elder girl slept every night with it under her pillow. For a while Kalpana could not speak. Renu went back to her mother, who stroked her face and said only, “You’ve come back—that is everything to me.”

Harimohan returned to his village and looked at the papers of his land in silence for a long time. Sanatan reached out to help him redeem the mortgaged field. Nirmal Babu and Savitri Devi went back to their families. They had thought they had become a burden because of their age; but the tears in their loved ones’ eyes taught them that their presence was the strength of the family.

For Malati and Renu, Sanatan started a small sewing unit in his workshop. Malati had her old sewing machine brought from the village; dust had settled in the folds of its cover, but as soon as the wheel turned, the familiar rattle returned. Slowly, work opened up for several other women as well. Nirmal Babu took charge of the account books. Savitri Devi sometimes came to the workshop kitchen and cooked for everyone. None of this was charity; each of them earned a living with dignity through their own skills.

When Arati’s mother Amrita set foot again on her own soil, Arati at first could not move forward. She had never remembered her mother; her mother’s face had only been a faint picture. Amrita looked at her daughter from a distance and said nothing; she only stretched out both arms. Slowly Arati went to her and took those hands. She remembered her father—the man who had chosen every darkness for this very moment, and could not live to see it.

But not everyone could go back to the life they had had. Gopal returned to find that his mother had died two months earlier for lack of medicine; he raised the shutter of his shop and stood silent for a long time. Nitai’s land had changed hands by then. In Madhu’s village people whispered, ‘Who knows what that boy did abroad’—in the end he moved to another town. Haripada came back, but woke with a start at night, and whenever a stranger spoke of a job his hands curled into fists.

Arati carried the grief of her father’s crimes. Sometimes she looked at his old photograph and wondered how a person could lose himself in such darkness. Sanatan never told her to forget everything. He knew that some wounds remain even with time. They decided to stand by each other. With the consent of their families, they were married. Their life together began with trust, honesty and a vow never to stay silent in the face of injustice.

Even after her marriage Arati kept Arun’s two letters carefully. About a year later an answer came through Interpol: the name of a young man called Arun had been on the list of a ship bound abroad; after that there was no further record. Arati read the letter, folded it and put it with the other two. She did not weep; she only held Sanatan’s hand a little tighter. Some questions are never answered, and one learns to live with that.

Sanatan resumed work on his bio-fuel project. His aim was to use agricultural waste and organic material to create the possibility of affordable fuel for rural areas. He did not keep the workshop only as a place of his own earning; he made it a place to learn skills as well.

One afternoon, standing by the workshop window, Sanatan thought of the old railway station. The day he had believed his life was over was the day everything had begun. Debu had saved him—but later he had become entangled in the web of Debu’s crimes. Life had taught him that accepting someone’s help does not mean mortgaging one’s conscience.

One day Amrita came to the workshop and sat beside Sanatan. He was looking out of the window. Amrita said gently, “The hand that pulled you up was the same hand that drowned you. Both are true. Don’t call one a lie because of the other.” Sanatan said, “Then how shall I remember him?” Amrita did not answer for a long time. Then she said, “As he was. My husband, my daughter’s father, and a criminal in the eyes of many. I have not forgiven him, Sanatan. But I do not want to spend the rest of my life in hatred either.”

Arati came and took his hand. The past had not been erased. Arun had still not been found, and the wait of many families was not over. Yet for those who had returned, a new morning had dawned. Sanatan had regained his name, his identity and the courage to live anew.

Amar was the name of his darkest chapter; Sanatan is the name of his rebirth.
— The End —
