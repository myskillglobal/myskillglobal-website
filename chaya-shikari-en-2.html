Second Chapter: Debu’s Mask

Debu’s house stood in the wealthy part of the city. A tall gate, a neat garden, foreign furniture in the sitting room—everything carried the mark of wealth. Flowering plants stood in rows before the house, costly lamps hung on the veranda, and inside lay soft carpets. There were servants, regular guests, boxes of foreign goods. Outsiders knew him as a successful businessman. Sometimes he spoke of import and export, sometimes of a business sending workers abroad.

Stepping out of the car, Amar—Sanatan, that is—stood looking at the house for a moment. He had never stayed in so large a house. Debu led him in and told an employee to show him a room. It was small but clean: a bed, a table, a cupboard, a chair by the window. Amar felt that after a long time he had found a safe place to lay his head.

New clothes, food, a little pocket money—everything seemed selfless help. “You are clever,” said Debu. “You will write down people’s names and addresses and put their papers in order. Slowly you will learn the work.” Amar was overwhelmed with gratitude. He thought that at last someone had trusted him. The next morning he was shown the office room, with a large table, filing cupboards, a typewriter, a telephone and several registers.

Debu explained the work. The names, ages, addresses, qualifications and identity-paper copies of people from various villages were to be arranged in separate files. Amar was told that people were recruited for jobs in factories, hotels, construction and security abroad. Some files held passport applications, some medical papers, some records of advance payments. Amar began to learn the work with care.

Debu’s daughter Arati was studying at college then. After her mother’s death her father was her only close one. She respected him, but she did not know every side of his business. She was curious about her father’s talks with guests, the comings and goings of foreigners, the sudden meetings at night. Arati had noticed that some visitors did not give their names when they came in, and that the servants opened a separate room for them.

One afternoon Arati came to the door of the office. Amar was writing names in a register. “Do you work in my father’s office?” she asked. Amar stood up. “Yes, I look after the papers for jobs abroad.” Arati glanced at some of the files. “So many people are sent abroad?” “Babu says it will give many people a chance of work,” said Amar. “Good for village boys.” Arati said nothing more, but the question stayed in her eyes.

That evening Arati was reading in the garden. Two middle-aged men came to the gate and spoke with the gatekeeper. After a while they were taken in. One carried an old bag; the other kept glancing around. They did not go to the sitting room but straight into her father’s private room. When Arati asked, Debu said, “Business people. You concentrate on your studies.” The words were ordinary, but his voice carried an unnecessary irritation.

Debu’s men searched the villages for unemployed youths. Some were promised factory jobs, some hotel work, some posts as security guards. At first came a small advance, good food, a place to stay—and trust was won. Anyone who brought a known person along was promised a commission. Poor families were told that a few months abroad would clear their debts. This is how the net spread.

Amar saw only the outer side of all this. He knew that many came in hope of work and that if papers were incomplete the journey abroad was delayed. Sometimes someone asked for money back, someone wanted to go home. Then an office employee would say, “The contract is signed, please wait a little.” Amar thought the rules of work abroad were perhaps simply that harsh.

But once people reached foreign lands, not everyone found work. Some vanished. Their families were told they had moved to another city or could not get in touch. In secret meetings the names of organs, the demands of hospitals and sums of money were discussed. Amar knew nothing of this; he was only asked to arrange names, ages, addresses and copies of documents. Some of the files he had arranged were later moved to another room.

One night, passing her father’s room, Arati heard someone say, “Too many questions are being asked about the last consignment. We must be careful in choosing people this time.” Debu replied in a low voice, “I am seeing to everything. Keep those whose papers have problems separate. Contact with their families must stay under control.” Arati stood still outside the door. The word ‘consignment’ sounded strange to her. Are human beings ever consigned?

The next morning Arati asked her father directly, “Why must your business keep people so secret?” Debu first laughed it off. Then he said, “There are many legal complications in sending people abroad. Not everyone will understand.” “Then explain it to me,” said Arati. Debu now answered in a hard voice, “You have your studies. Don’t trouble your head about business.”

Meanwhile Amar went on working, filled with gratitude to Debu. Sometimes he remembered home. One day he asked Debu whether money had been sent to his family. Debu reassured him: “It has been arranged. Now learn your work.” Amar did not press him.

He did not realise that the man who had brought him back from the mouth of death was now using him to push others towards danger. The seed of suspicion that had been planted in Arati’s mind would, in time, become the cause of a great storm.
