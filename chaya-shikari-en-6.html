Sixth Chapter: Love and Betrayal

One evening on the veranda of the house Arati asked Amar, “Is your name really Amar?” After a long silence, Amar told her about his past—standing by the railway line, deciding to throw himself in front of the train, being saved by Debu and receiving a new identity. Arati listened closely. She understood how deep the bonds of gratitude and debt ran in Amar’s life.

“You think of that man as the giver of your life,” said Arati, “but I’m afraid of him. There are things my father does that I cannot understand.” Amar wanted to protest at first, but then he remembered the medical tests at the guesthouse. A strange silence fell between them. Love had been born between them, but before that love stood a hard question about the truth.

The next morning Arati stood at the workshop door with a torn sandal in her hand. “I’m going to the guesthouse today,” she said. “Father is not in town.” Amar set down his tool. “Don’t go. What if you’re caught?” “Then you will rescue me,” Arati said with a laugh, though the laugh trembled. Amar looked at her for a while. Then he said, “Give me the sandal.”

With a bit of fine wire from the workshop he stitched the strap firm. As he worked he taught her in a low voice, “If the guard stops you, say you came to see about the guests’ food. Don’t hold anyone’s eyes for long. And…” He stopped. “And?” “And come back.” Arati slipped on the sandal. At the door she looked back. “You are more afraid than I am.” “Yes,” said Amar, honestly. This time Arati really smiled.

Debu was out of town that day. Taking the chance, Arati went to the guesthouse; since she was the owner’s daughter, the guard did not stop her. She said she had come to check on the guests.

In one corner of the veranda Kalpana sat with a folded, grimy piece of paper in her lap. Seeing Arati, she quickly covered it with her sari, then, thinking better of it, took it out again. “Didi, will you do something for me?” Her voice was so low she seemed afraid of her own words. “My elder girl has learnt her letters now. I have written, ‘Mother is well, keep up your studies.’ But no one is allowed to send letters here. I asked three times; they only said, tomorrow.”

Arati took the paper. The writing was clumsy, the ink blotted in two places—from tears or sweat, one could not tell. Below the address was a tiny drawing: a house, and two figures in front of it. “The younger one can’t read yet,” said Kalpana, “so I drew a picture. So she will know her mother hasn’t forgotten them.”

Arati could not say anything. She tucked the paper inside her blouse. On the way out the guard at the gate glanced at her once. Her heart was pounding so hard that she thought everyone must hear it. The guard only said, “Didimoni, don’t stay long—the master gets angry.” Outside the gate Arati let out her first breath. The next day she posted the letter with her own hands.

That day Renu too had said she had been told she would work in a hotel, yet here she could not go anywhere. Savitri Devi, clutching her bundle, had said, “My people at home think I’m learning a job. What if I can’t go back?”

Arati understood: these were not workers; they were being held like prisoners. She went back to her father’s room to search for papers. In a hidden compartment of a cupboard she found a thick envelope. Inside were lists of names, with health examination details, names of organs and sums of money beside them. The names of Malati, Kalpana, Renu, Nirmal Babu, Harimohan and Savitri Devi were on the list. Beside some other names were dates of departure abroad.

One name made Arati’s hand tremble—Arun. Arun had once been the man she loved. Debu had sent him abroad with a promise of good earnings. Since then there had been no news of him. All this time Arati had thought he must have started a new life. Today she understood that there might be a terrible secret behind his disappearance.

On another page of the envelope was Amar’s own name. He was the next possible victim. Arati showed the papers to Amar, who broke down. The people he had assured of work might be in mortal danger. “I didn’t know,” he said. “But not knowing doesn’t end my responsibility.”

That night, in the lamplight of the workshop, Amar turned over the papers from the envelope one by one. Beside many names the handwriting was his own—he had arranged them, he had written ‘health good’ next to the addresses. Touching his own writing with a finger, he said, “These letters are mine. Every one of them is my responsibility.” Arati laid her hand over his. “If you take responsibility, then it can be repaid,” she said. “Not if you run away.”

The two decided to hand the evidence to the police. First they went to an officer they knew in the district police. He listened carefully and arranged for them to spend the night at a safe place.

But in the middle of the night, at the sound of a car outside the window, Amar woke. Through a gap in the curtain he saw a black car stop at the mouth of the lane and several men get out. “Arati, get up,” he whispered. Only a few people should have known the address of this refuge. Someone had leaked their location.

They slipped out by the back door and ran along the ridges between the paddy fields. The soil was slippery from the previous night’s rain, and the strap of Arati’s sandal—which Amar had stitched with wire—snapped; she kept running barefoot. Amar held the envelope pressed against his chest inside his shirt. Behind them torchlight swung again and again, and the barking of dogs drew closer. They lay for an hour in a dry drain, holding their breath.

Before dawn they changed their route, reached the station and caught a train—the very same station where Amar had once tried to end his life. Stepping onto the platform, he stopped for a moment. Arati asked nothing; she only held his hand tightly. At the CID office, the moment he laid the first page of the envelope on the table, the duty officer’s expression changed. Now the wheels of the investigation truly began to turn.
