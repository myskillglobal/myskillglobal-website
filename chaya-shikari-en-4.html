Fourth Chapter: Sixty Names and a Secret Ledger

The guesthouse stood far from the city, like an old garden house. A high wall outside, an iron gate in front, several large rooms within. Once the house had been ringed with mango and jackfruit trees; now little was visible but weeds and broken paths. Those who were to go abroad were kept there before departure. It was said that once the paperwork was done everyone would leave. But even as days went by, no one was allowed outside.

Among those brought there were Malati, a woman who had run her household by stitching since her husband died, and who had been told she would work in a garment factory abroad; Kalpana, a mother of two, who had come hoping for work as a nursing assistant; and Renu, who had been promised a job in a hotel. Nirmal Babu, a retired man, had spent a large part of his savings hoping for a security guard’s post. Harimohan had mortgaged his land in the hope of farm work abroad. And Savitri Devi had been told of a cook’s job in an old-age home.

On the first day everyone thought they would not have to stay more than a few nights. But a week went by. Meals came on time, yet there was no permission to go out. Even when they spoke to family on the phone, a guard stood beside them. If anyone asked, they were told, “Agency rules. Until the papers are complete, no one can go out.” Slowly fear gathered in everyone’s mind.

One evening some strangers came to Debu’s house. Amar was arranging papers in the next room; the door was not fully closed. He heard talk of the demands of several hospitals abroad, the names of organs, and huge sums of money. One man said, “The reports of a few on the earlier list are not good.” Debu answered, “Then keep replacements ready. Finish the papers first for those whose reports are fine.”

Unease gathered in Amar’s chest. What did a hospital discussion have to do with jobs abroad? He tried to keep his eyes on the paper but read the same line again and again without understanding. He started to ask a question and stopped. He was in Debu’s debt; if he displeased him he might have to go back onto the road. He still had not recovered his old identity either.

The next day health examinations were arranged for several people. Amar stood with papers in his hand. One person’s blood group was noted, another’s X-ray. One man showed signs of TB and was dropped from the list. Two men spoke separately about an eye-test report. At the word ‘corneal transplant’ the sound seemed to lodge in Amar’s ear. He had never before seen such tests for a job.

Checking the list, he grew even more astonished. Why were kidney and liver tests being done for Nirmal Babu, who was going as a security guard? Why was so much importance given to the blood group of Savitri Devi, going to cook in an old-age home? In Malati’s file there was mention of yet another kind of test. Once Amar had thought perhaps that was simply the rule abroad. But seeing a different organ examined for each person, his suspicion thickened.

He gathered courage and asked Debu, “Babu, why so many kinds of tests for a job? Nirmal Babu’s kidney and liver, Savitri Devi’s blood group—are these needed for a visa?” Debu smiled. “Foreign rules. Without good health no visa. In many countries the employers have everything tested beforehand. You do your work.” His answer sounded very simple, but a shadow of irritation lay in his eyes.

That night Nirmal Babu called Amar near the veranda and said, “Son, I have never seen so many tests before a job. I am old, true, but I am going to do security work. What will they do with my kidney and liver?” Amar found no answer. Nirmal Babu sighed. “I promised my grandson that with my first pay I would meet his school costs. If I go back now, there will be no land, nothing.”

Savitri Devi sat a little way off. She said softly, “I told my son that I would send money once I got work. But since coming here my heart feels uneasy. When I want to phone, they say later. My boy must be worried.” Kalpana began to cry as she spoke of her two children. Malati comforted her but could not hide her own tears. Looking at them, Amar understood: none of these people was a criminal; each had come hoping to live a little better.

For the first time Amar realised that he might not have merely arranged papers; he had pushed people’s fates towards a door whose other side he did not know. That night he could not sleep. Each name in the ledger seemed to look at him and ask, “Where are you sending us?” He resolved in his mind not to ignore any further doubt. But how to learn the truth, and whom to trust—he had no answer.

Arati too had noticed something unnatural in her father’s behaviour. Keeping people shut in the guesthouse, limiting their contact with their families, the sudden health tests—taken together, her suspicion deepened. One day she saw an envelope lying on her father’s table. On it were several names and numbers, with strange abbreviations beside them. Arati was about to open it, then stopped; at that very moment her father’s footsteps sounded in the room. She moved away quickly.

That night Amar sat in his room thinking of the files. Outside, the guard’s footsteps; far away, a dog barking. It seemed to him that every door in this house kept some secret shut inside. He did not know that Arati too was searching for a thread in the same darkness. Their suspicions ran along separate paths, but the truth about to be revealed to them concerned not only Debu’s business—it concerned the lives of many innocent people.
