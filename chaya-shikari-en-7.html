Seventh Chapter: The End of Debu

The CID first verified the documents. The lists of names, health reports, financial accounts and details of foreign contacts were cross-checked against one another. The information provided by Richard Hamilton also became an important lead. Amar did not hide his own role. He explained how he had been used to collect names and arrange papers.

The investigators realised that time was short. A plan to move several people elsewhere was under way. Amar told them everything he knew about the guesthouse, its guards and the movements of Debu’s men. Arati told them about the secret room in her father’s house and the places where documents were kept.

On the eve of the operation Arati said to Amar, “I want to face my father once. I want to know why.” Amar did not stop her; he too wanted an answer to that question. The two entered the house quietly. Debu sat in his study. On the table lay a packed bag, beside it a lamp burning low. The secret shelf in the cupboard stood empty—one look at it and he seemed to understand everything.

“So it was you two,” Debu said in a calm voice. He did not shout, nor did he call anyone. He only said, “Sit. Listen to my story today. Then do what you must.” Arati remained standing. Debu looked at the wall and said, “You were told that your mother died of fever. That was a lie. Your mother’s name was Amrita.”

“You were very small then. A man came and told Amrita there was a teacher’s job in a girls’ school abroad, good pay. I was away on work. When I came back the house was empty. I followed the trail of that agent, searching, and reached a foreign land. It took several months to find him. They had sent your mother to a baijee’s house. I offered money, I fell at their feet, they would not let her go. They said a woman who knew so much was too risky to keep alive. She would have been killed.”

Debu’s voice shook. “I begged, I pleaded for her life. They said, we will keep her alive—if you work for us. Gather people, fill the ships. Your mother is still held there, Arati. Each time they said, one more ship, then we will release her. If I had sent this last ship, they truly would have let her go.” Saying this, he covered his face with both hands and broke down.

After a long time Amar asked, “That day at the station you saved me. Was it then that you thought of me as the next victim?” Debu was silent for a long time. “I pulled the lever because a boy was about to die. I thought of nothing else then. But sitting in the car, I began to calculate. Whether pity came first or the calculation—I still don’t know, Amar. I never even had the courage to ask myself.”

Arati had been silent all this while. Now she asked in a dry voice, “Arun?” Debu could not meet his daughter’s eyes. “I sent him saying, when he comes back with money I will think of your marriage. I did not know then—or did I not want to know?—I cannot separate even that today. I have no last news of him, Arati. That is my greatest shame.”

Then he took a small notebook from the table drawer and put it in Arati’s hand. “All the addresses abroad are in here—the agents’, the docks’, and the house where your mother is. If I am caught, they will know at the very first news. Then they will either move your mother or finish her, so that there is no witness. Do not give this notebook to the local police; their people are there too. Give it to the CID chief. Tell him to work quietly through Interpol.”

“Father, come with us. You can tell everything in court,” Arati said, weeping. Debu laid a hand on his daughter’s head. “If I go, they will know before tomorrow morning. In trying to save your mother, I have bought the tears of thousands of mothers, my child. This debt cannot be forgiven. Go, don’t delay. Let me be alone a little.” Arati paused at the door and looked back once. Debu was gazing at the dying light of the lamp. Holding Amar’s hand, she came out, the notebook pressed to her heart.

On the appointed night the police and the CID raided Debu’s house and the guesthouse together. When the guesthouse gate opened, the people inside were afraid at first. They thought they were being taken somewhere else again. An officer introduced himself and said, “You are safe. We will arrange for you to go home.” Kalpana burst into tears. Renu stood by the door as if she could not believe it. Savitri Devi kept asking, “You will send word to my home, won’t you?”

Malati, Kalpana, Renu, Nirmal Babu, Harimohan, Savitri Devi and several others were rescued. Arrangements were made for their medical care, the verification of their identities and contact with their families. The investigators understood that these people were not merely witnesses—they were potential victims of a large criminal network.

But before the police could enter, everything was already over. Debu was found dead in the study; he had ended his own life. Arati broke down at the news. She had loved her father, but she could not support his crimes. And she understood that on that last evening he had not only chosen his own punishment—he had also handed over the one path that could save her mother’s life.

The CID chief took the notebook and for several days did not let news of Debu’s death out. Contact was made secretly, through Interpol, with the authorities abroad. One night the addresses were raided simultaneously. After many years Amrita was rescued from that captivity, along with several other women.

Debu’s death did not stop the investigation. Following the recovered documents, the notebook and the financial trails, the agents were identified. Contact with the relevant authorities continued in the search for those sent abroad. The whereabouts of Arun and others who had vanished earlier were still unknown. Even amid the joy of those rescued, that uncertainty remained.
