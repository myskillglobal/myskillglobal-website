The city was busy. In the daylight, its streets were crowded with people, car horns, and the noise of shops — life seemed to rush along at its own pace. Yet in one corner of that same city, the lives of four young men seemed to have come to a standstill.

Ayan, Rahul, Saikat, and Pradip — the four had been friends since college. All four were highly educated, talented, and had once dreamed big about their futures.

One would get a good job, one would start a business, one would go abroad — how many plans they had made!

But once their studies ended, real life showed them a completely different picture.

One job exam after another.

One interview after another.

And then, always, the same answer—

"You have not been selected."

At first they were disheartened, but they didn't give up. Ayan searched the newspaper for job ads every day. Saikat applied through various companies' websites. Rahul somehow covered his own expenses by giving private tuition. And Pradip wandered from place to place, day after day, chasing work.

But the months went by.

No job came.

One day Ayan's father, exasperated, said,

— "What good did all that studying do? You can't even bring a single rupee into this household!"

Ayan had no answer.

He hung his head and went back to his room.

Saikat's family wasn't well off either. His mother was ill. Sometimes he had to borrow from a friend just to afford her medicine.

Rahul's father used to say, again and again,

— "You're a quiet boy, a sharp boy. You'll surely land a good job."

But behind his father's hopeful words, Rahul could feel the family's money troubles growing.

And Pradip?

His nature was a little different.

Angry, stubborn, impatient.

Failure after failure was making him harder by the day.

• • •

One evening the four friends sat at an old tea stall in the city.

Four cups of tea sat on the table. But no one said a word.

In the end, it was Pradip who broke the silence.

— "How much longer?"

Rahul looked up.

— "What?"

— "How much longer does this go on? No job, no money, humiliated at home every single day. Is there really no place in this society for boys like us?"

Saikat said slowly,

— "I am trying, at least."

Pradip laughed bitterly.

— "Trying? How many more years will you try? Until your own family drags you out by the collar?"

Ayan sat quietly, staring at his cup of tea.

Then he said, slowly,

— "We have to find a way out."

Hearing that, the other three looked at him.

That evening they talked for a long time.

Jobs, business, loans — every possibility came up one by one. But every path seemed to end at a locked door.

Finally Pradip said, in a low voice,

— "There's one thing that could get us a lot of money, very fast."

Rahul frowned.

— "What thing?"

Pradip glanced around and leaned in.

Then he said something that meant the four friends' lives would never be the same again.

— "Do you know the city's industrialist, Ashok Agarwal?"

Ayan said,

— "I know him. Huge business. He's even got a big factory outside the city."

Pradip said,

— "He has one daughter. Six years old."

Rahul felt his chest tighten.

— "And then?"

Pradip said, in a cold voice,

— "What if we took that girl away, just for a few days...?"

Before he could even finish, Rahul stood up from his chair.

— "Have you lost your mind?"

Pradip only looked back at him.

— "Why?"

— "Kidnap a small child? That's a crime! Do you even understand what you're saying?"

Ayan too was growing uneasy.

Saikat said nothing.

But now Pradip started making his case.

— "We won't hurt anyone. We'll just keep her a few days. Then we'll take the money and let her go. A few crore rupees is nothing to Ashok Agarwal."

Rahul said, in a hard voice,

— "Since when does not having money make committing a crime acceptable?"

Pradip fell silent.

After a while, Ayan said,

— "Let's go home for tonight. Never say this again."

The four of them got up.

• • •

But none of them slept properly that night.

Ayan kept turning the same thought over — could they really sink that low?

Before Saikat's eyes floated the image of his mother's face.

Rahul kept telling himself—

"Whatever happens, I won't let any harm come to a child."

And Pradip?

He stood by the window, staring out at the dark city.

In his mind there was only one thought—

"Once we have the money, every problem will be solved."

But none of them knew that, very soon, a little six-year-old girl would step right into the middle of their dark plan.

And that little girl would go on to change all four of their lives.

Part 2

The First Meeting with Simpa

Back to Contents ↑

After a great deal of hesitation, the four friends made a plan, one day, to take Simpa. But even they themselves didn't know what they'd do after that.

When their chance came and they stood before Simpa, the six-year-old wasn't scared at all. Instead, she looked up at the four of them with wide eyes and said,

— "Where are you taking me?"

Pradip said, a little sternly,

— "You'll have to stay with us for a few days."

Simpa asked, surprised,

— "Why?"

This time Rahul couldn't stay quiet. He sat down in front of Simpa and said gently,

— "We're in a lot of trouble, sweetheart. None of us have jobs. There's a lot of hardship at home. We're suffering badly, because of money."

Simpa listened carefully.

Then, in her small voice, she said,

— "Then why are you all crying?"

The four friends looked at each other.

Ayan sighed and said,

— "We're about to do something wrong."

Simpa was quiet for a moment, then said,

— "But my father has so much money!"

Pradip said,

— "That's exactly why..."

But he couldn't finish the sentence.

Simpa suddenly said,

— "I'll come with you."

Rahul was startled.

— "You want to come with us?"

Simpa nodded.

— "Yes. My father has so much money, and because of it, he doesn't treat poor people well."

All four of them were stunned.

Simpa went on, in the plain, simple way of a child,

— "One day an uncle came to ask Baba for money. Baba scolded him terribly. The uncle was crying."

She paused, then said again,

— "And an old grandfather came too. Baba's men threw him out as well."

Rahul's face fell.

Simpa said now,

— "Baba needs to be taught a lesson."

Pradip had been standing there hard-faced all this while. Hearing this, something like a smile flickered across his face too.

But hidden behind that smile, no one could tell — this little girl was, very soon, about to change all four of their lives.

And in Rahul's mind, a single question kept circling—

"Are we really about to make such a terrible mistake, with this innocent child?"

Part 3

The Room Fear Could Not Enter

Back to Contents ↑

Simpa's first night at the hideout passed strangely.

The four friends had imagined that a little six-year-old girl, dropped into unfamiliar surroundings, would be frightened and cry. That she'd keep insisting on going back to her father.

But Simpa seemed to be made of different stuff altogether.

After dinner, she looked from Ayan to Rahul, to Saikat, to Pradip, and said,

— "The four of you are friends?"

Ayan nodded.

— "Yes."

— "Then now I have four friends too!"

All four of them fell silent.

No one could bring themselves to say — "We're not your friends. We brought you here unjustly."

Rahul only said,

— "Yes, we're here with you."

Simpa lay down, her mind at ease.

Before long, the soft, sweet sound of her sleeping breath could be heard.

But there was no sleep left in the four friends' eyes.

Ayan whispered,

— "She trusts us so easily!"

Saikat sighed.

— "Can you believe what a terrible thing we've done?"

Pradip said nothing at all.

He just kept looking at the sleeping Simpa.

The Next Morning

Sunlight slipped in through the window and woke Simpa.

She opened her eyes and looked around.

Then she climbed out of bed and said,

— "Good morning!"

Rahul was getting breakfast ready.

He smiled and said,

— "Good morning. What will you have?"

Simpa thought for a moment and said,

— "Whatever you're having, I'll have that too."

A smile spread across Rahul's face.

— "All right, then. Khichuri it is."

Simpa clapped her hands.

— "I love khichuri!"

Meanwhile Pradip, unable to find something in the room, had grown irritated.

— "Where did it go? No one ever puts anything back where it belongs!"

At the sound of his voice, Ayan and Saikat both went quiet.

But Simpa wasn't the least bit afraid.

She walked slowly up to Pradip, took his rough hands in hers, and said,

— "You don't look nice at all when you're angry. Won't you smile a little?"

Pradip went still, as if caught completely off guard.

He looked at Simpa.

All this time, whenever people saw his temper, they'd stepped away. And yet this little girl, instead of fearing his anger, was telling him to smile!

Pradip turned his face away.

— "I... was just angry for no reason."

Simpa said sweetly,

— "Then don't be angry anymore."

A smile crept onto the corner of Pradip's lips, without him even noticing.

Simpa clapped and said,

— "There you go! Now you look nice."

Rahul watched the whole scene while he cooked.

There was a strange tenderness in his eyes.

Four New Friends

As soon as breakfast was over, Simpa found an old ludo board tucked in a corner of the room.

— "What's this?"

Ayan said,

— "Ludo."

— "Will you play?"

The four of them looked at each other.

In the end, everyone sat down.

A little while later, the whole mood of the room had changed.

When Simpa won, she clapped.

When she lost, she sat there pouting.

When Pradip let her win on purpose, she'd say happily,

— "Pradip-da is the best!"

Pradip kept his face stern, but inside, he was melting.

In the afternoon Simpa listened to a story from Ayan.

She learned to draw a picture from Saikat.

And after eating Rahul's cooking, she announced,

— "Rahul-da cooks the best!"

There was a smile on all four faces.

But hidden beneath that smile was guilt.

Because they knew — this happy little place wasn't really a child's home.

The Afternoon Question

In the afternoon Simpa sat by the window, looking outside.

Suddenly she said,

— "Is my father looking for me?"

Hearing the question, all four of them fell quiet.

Rahul said slowly,

— "Yes. He must be looking for you."

— "Is Baba crying because of me?"

Rahul's throat tightened.

Ayan lowered his head.

Saikat stared out the window.

Pradip too turned his face away, this time.

Simpa was quiet for a while, then said,

— "I love my father."

That single sentence seemed to press an even heavier stone onto all four of their chests.

Rahul said to himself—

"How much longer can we keep this child away from her father?"

That Night's Conversation

That night, once Simpa was asleep, the four friends sat together again.

Rahul said,

— "We have to decide something."

Ayan said,

— "Decide what?"

Rahul said firmly,

— "We won't take any money."

Saikat looked up.

— "Meaning?"

— "We'll give Simpa back."

Silence settled over the room.

Pradip had been quiet the whole while.

He said, slowly,

— "You're right."

Ayan looked at him, surprised.

— "You agree?"

Pradip stared for a while at Simpa's sleeping face.

Then he said,

— "After seeing her, I can't ask for money anymore."

Rahul said,

— "And after that?"

Pradip sighed.

— "After that, whatever happens, happens. If we have to go to the police, we go."

Saikat said,

— "Are we going to turn ourselves in?"

For a while, no one spoke.

Then Ayan nodded.

— "Yes. Since we made the mistake, the blame is ours to carry too."

Right at that moment, Simpa's sleepy voice came from the next room—

— "Rahul-da..."

Rahul got up quickly.

Simpa, half-asleep, reached out a hand and said,

— "Are you there?"

Rahul sat beside her, took her hand, and said,

— "I'm here."

Simpa drifted back into an easy sleep.

Rahul looked at her and said to himself—

"Tomorrow I'll take you back to your father, Simpa."

But the next morning, just as they set out to act on that decision, something would happen — something that would make it far harder to keep.

Part 4

A City-Wide Search

Back to Contents ↑

That night became the longest night of Ashok Agarwal and his wife's lives.

Lights blazed through the vast rooms of the house, security guards ran back and forth, the police kept asking question after question — but nowhere was there any trace of Simpa.

Ashok's wife kept repeating the same words,

— "Find my daughter. Tell me where she is!"

Tears streamed down her face as she spoke.

Ashok was, ordinarily, an extremely strong-willed man. In the business world, everyone knew him for his tough decisions. But that day, even that man had broken down.

Again and again he walked into his daughter's room.

Simpa's little bed sat empty.

Her favourite doll lay on the table.

Colour pencils in one corner.

An unfinished drawing of Simpa's on the wall.

Ashok stood still, staring at the drawing.

Waiting for a Call

The night wore on.

A fear kept growing steadily stronger in Ashok's mind.

If it's a kidnapping, surely a call will come.

A ransom will be demanded.

How much money, where to deliver it — all of it will be spelled out.

But the night passed.

No call came.

Not a single one.

No unknown number.

No message.

No ransom demand at all.

Ashok kept glancing, again and again, at his phone.

At one point his wife said, through tears,

— "Why isn't anyone calling?"

Ashok had no answer.

He only said,

— "I don't know."

His wife gripped his hand tightly and said,

— "If they ask for money, we'll pay it. Whatever it takes, we'll pay. Just let them bring my daughter back."

Ashok fell silent again.

In his mind, an even more terrifying question kept circling—

"If money isn't the point, then why did they take Simpa at all?"

He had no answer to that question.

At the Police Station

The next morning, Ashok went back to the police station.

The investigating officer asked,

— "Still no ransom call?"

Ashok shook his head.

— "No."

— "Any threats?"

— "No."

— "Any message at all?"

— "Nothing."

The police officers grew uneasy too.

In most kidnapping cases, the criminals reach out to the family in one way or another. But here — total silence.

The investigating officer said,

— "It's this very silence that worries us the most."

Ashok said, in a shaking voice,

— "You won't let any harm come to my daughter — will you?"

The officer said calmly,

— "We're looking into every possible angle."

The Detectives Investigate

The next day, a special investigation team arrived at Ashok's house.

The team's experienced officer, Arindam Sen, began by examining Simpa's room closely.

He noted everything — her toys, her books, her drawing notebook.

Then he asked Ashok,

— "Do you have any enemies?"

Ashok said,

— "I have business rivals. But I can't imagine anyone doing this to my daughter."

The detective asked again,

— "Any major dispute with anyone recently?"

Ashok was quiet for a moment.

— "A few employees came to me looking for help. Some wanted work too. But..."

— "But?"

— "You can't help everyone."

The detective wrote something in his notebook.

Then he said,

— "Is there anyone connected to your business who's in financial difficulty?"

Ashok gave a few names.

The investigators noted them down.

A City-Wide Search

Simpa's photo was sent out to various police stations.

Searches began across key areas of the city.

The police started speaking with possible witnesses.

But no solid lead turned up.

And the strangest thing of all—

the kidnappers had left behind no trace whatsoever.

No demand.

No call.

No threat.

No mention of ransom, none at all.

Ashok's fear kept growing, day by day.

He tried to comfort his wife, even as he himself was falling apart inside.

Meanwhile...

At a quiet edge of the city, in that house, the four friends were caught in an entirely different state of mind.

Simpa woke up that morning and asked, as naturally as ever,

— "What will we eat today?"

Rahul forced a smile.

— "Khichuri."

— "Yay!"

Simpa clapped.

But there was no smile on any of the four friends' faces.

Ayan came back from outside and said,

— "The police are searching the city."

Saikat lowered his head.

Pradip let out a sigh.

— "We're not making any call."

Rahul looked at him.

— "There's no question of calling anyone. We're not asking for money."

Ayan said,

— "But what if the police find us?"

Rahul said calmly,

— "If they find us, so be it. But we can't keep Simpa here even one moment longer."

Simpa was sitting nearby, arranging her ludo pieces.

Suddenly she looked up and said,

— "You'll take me to Baba, won't you?"

Rahul's eyes welled up.

— "Yes, Simpa."

Simpa smiled.

— "Then I'll tell Baba you all took really good care of me."

The four friends looked at one another.

Their guilt grew heavier still.

But they had no idea—

at Ashok Agarwal's house, the investigators had just come across a piece of information that could open the first door into this mystery.

And that lead would, in the end, take them straight to four unemployed, talented young men.

Part 5

Facing the Truth

Back to Contents ↑

That day, detective Arindam Sen sat at Ashok Agarwal's house, piecing together one clue after another.

The strangest thing about Simpa's disappearance was — no one had made any ransom demand.

Arindam said,

— "I don't think this is an ordinary kidnapping."

Ashok asked, worried,

— "Then what could it be?"

— "I can't say for certain yet. But we're looking into your personal life as much as your business connections."

Ashok said, a little annoyed,

— "What does my personal life have to do with my daughter going missing?"

Arindam said calmly,

— "Often the motive isn't money at all — it can be some other grievance."

Hearing that, Ashok fell silent.

An Uncomfortable Piece of Information

The investigators spoke with several of Ashok's employees.

One old employee hesitated, then said,

— "Sir, may I say something?"

— "Go ahead."

— "A while back, a few educated young men came looking for work. They'd studied very well. But sir spoke to them very harshly."

Arindam's brow furrowed.

— "What did he say to them?"

The man hesitated a little.

— "I don't remember exactly. But the boys left feeling deeply humiliated."

Another employee said,

— "It's happened before too."

Arindam noted down a few names.

The investigation took a new turn.

The Four Friends' Decision

Meanwhile, at the hideout, the four friends sat together one last time.

Simpa was in the next room, playing with her doll.

Rahul said,

— "We have to take her back today."

Ayan nodded.

— "Yes."

Saikat said,

— "But should we drop her right at her doorstep?"

Pradip was quiet for a while, then said,

— "Whatever it takes, Simpa needs to reach her father safely."

Rahul said,

— "And after that?"

Pradip sighed.

— "After that, we don't run."

Ayan looked at him.

— "Meaning?"

— "We turn ourselves in to the police."

Silence filled the room.

Rahul said slowly,

— "That's the right thing to do."

Simpa's Question

Simpa came over to them while playing.

— "Are you going somewhere?"

Rahul rested a hand on her head and said,

— "Yes. We're taking you home."

Simpa said, happily,

— "Baba will be so happy to see me."

Then, after a moment's thought, she said,

— "But won't you come with me?"

Rahul's eyes fell.

— "We have something we need to take care of."

Simpa didn't understand.

She looked at all four of them and said,

— "You're not bad people."

Hearing that, Pradip suddenly turned away.

Tears had risen to his eyes.

He wiped his face quickly.

Ashok's Unease

Around that time, the investigators brought Ashok Agarwal a list.

There were a few names on it.

Among them were Ayan, Rahul, Saikat, and Pradip.

Ashok stared at the names for a while.

Suddenly his expression changed.

— "These boys..."

Arindam asked,

— "You know them?"

Ashok said, slowly,

— "Yes. A few months back, they came to my office."

— "Looking for work?"

— "Yes."

— "And then?"

Ashok went quiet.

Arindam said,

— "Did you treat them badly?"

Hearing the question, Ashok's face grew heavy.

After a moment, he said,

— "Perhaps... I was too harsh."

Arindam said,

— "What did you say to them?"

Ashok gave no answer.

He only kept looking at his daughter's photograph.

The Way Back

Just before evening fell, the four friends set out with Simpa.

Simpa was strangely happy that day.

She kept saying,

— "I'm going to tell Baba something."

Rahul asked,

— "What?"

— "I'll tell him you all took really good care of me."

Ayan and Saikat both turned their faces away at once.

Pradip kept staring out the car window.

Rahul looked at Simpa and said to himself,

"You didn't turn us into criminals by coming into our lives, Simpa. You showed us — we can still become good men again."

Meanwhile, at the Police Station

On detective Arindam Sen's desk lay a photograph of the four friends.

He said to an officer,

— "Find them."

The officer asked,

— "Is their connection to the kidnapping confirmed?"

Arindam said,

— "Not yet."

Then, after a pause, he added,

— "But here's the strange part — if they really do have Simpa, why haven't they demanded ransom in all this time?"

No one had an answer to that question yet.

But that answer was about to surface very soon.

Because the four friends had already made their decision—

they would bring Simpa back and turn themselves in to the police.

And that meeting would change the lives of Ashok Agarwal, Simpa, and the four friends, forever.

Final Part

The Door to a New Life

Back to Contents ↑

Evening had fallen by then.

A car pulled up, suddenly, in front of Ashok Agarwal's huge house.

Rahul stepped out first.

Then Ayan, Saikat, and Pradip.

And last of all, little Simpa.

The moment Simpa saw the house, she cried out in joy,

— "Ma!"

The door flew open and Ashok's wife came running out.

She pulled her daughter into her arms and broke down in tears.

— "Simpa! My darling! Where were you all this time?"

Simpa hugged her mother back and said,

— "I was fine, Ma."

Then, holding her mother's hand, she pointed at the four young men and said,

— "They took very good care of me."

Ashok stood a little way off.

Tears had come to his eyes at the joy of getting his daughter back. But the moment he laid eyes on the four young men, his face hardened.

The house's security guards moved forward.

But Rahul raised a hand and stopped them.

— "There's no need to do anything to them."

Then, turning to Ashok, he said,

— "We made a terrible mistake. We had no right to take Simpa."

Ayan said, head bowed,

— "We never asked for a single rupee."

Saikat said,

— "We just... went down the wrong path."

Pradip had stayed silent all this while.

He stepped forward and said,

— "The fault is mine, more than anyone's. I'm the one who dragged everyone into this."

Then, all four of them said together,

— "We've come to surrender to the police."

Ashok's Question

Ashok looked at them for a long moment.

Then he said,

— "If you didn't want money, then why did you take my daughter?"

Ayan said, head lowered,

— "We'd been unemployed for a long time. Money troubles at home, insults, constant unrest... we grew weak."

Rahul said,

— "But after we took Simpa, we understood just how huge our mistake was."

Ashok looked at Simpa.

— "What did they do to you?"

Simpa spoke up at once,

— "They played with me!"

Ashok was stunned.

Simpa began listing it all off,

— "Ayan-da told me stories. Saikat-da drew pictures with me. Rahul-da made khichuri and fed me."

Then, looking at Pradip, she smiled and said,

— "And Pradip-da is really grumpy. I told him he doesn't look nice when he's angry. And then he'd smile!"

Pradip stood there, head down.

Ashok's eyes settled firmly on the four faces before him.

He understood — these weren't professional criminals.

They had made a terrible mistake.

But even within that mistake, over these past days, they had genuinely looked after his daughter.

And most important of all—

they had never once asked for ransom.

They could have run, if they'd wanted to.

Instead they had brought Simpa back themselves, and had come ready to surrender to the police.

Ashok's Realization

Ashok slowly sat down in a chair.

Memories of his own past began to surface.

How many people had come to him seeking work.

How many poor people had come to him for help.

How many times he had turned them away.

How many young men had walked into his office hoping for a job — and he had never even given them the time to speak.

He looked at the four friends.

— "You'd come to me looking for a job?"

Ayan nodded.

— "Yes."

— "Did I insult you?"

All four of them fell silent.

The silence itself became the answer.

Ashok let out a long, heavy breath.

— "Then the crime is on you. But some of the responsibility for how you got here is on me, too."

Rahul looked up, surprised.

Ashok said,

— "What you did isn't forgivable. But the fact that you brought my daughter back yourselves, and came to stand before the police — I can't overlook that either."

He was quiet for a moment.

Then he said,

— "I want to give you a chance."

A New Test

Ashok told the police the whole story.

He made it clear: the four young men had safely returned his daughter, and had never demanded any ransom.

He understood the legal process would run its own course. But he decided not to take the harshest possible stance against them, and instead to help them, within the bounds of the law, as much as he reasonably could.

Then he said to the four of them,

— "If you truly want to change your lives, then prove it through a test."

All four were taken aback.

Ashok said,

— "I won't hand you a job out of pity."

— "Then?"

— "You'll have to prove you deserve it."

The next day, a special examination was arranged at his company.

A written test.

Problem-solving.

Technical skills.

Logic and analysis.

Then an interview.

All four of them gave it everything they had.

On the day the results came out, Ashok called them in himself.

He said, smiling,

— "All four of you have passed."

Tears filled their eyes.

Pradip said quietly,

— "Sir, do we really deserve this chance?"

Ashok said,

— "You've proved your ability in the exam. Now prove it in your lives."

A Few Months Later

Time moved on.

Ayan began working in an important department of the company.

Rahul's skill quickly caught everyone's attention.

Saikat proved himself in the technical division.

And Pradip?

The boy who once used to lose his temper over the smallest thing slowly learned to master his anger.

One day, walking out of the office, the four friends strolled along side by side.

Pradip suddenly said,

— "Remember how all of this started?"

Saikat laughed.

— "How could I forget?"

Ayan said,

— "The biggest mistake of our lives."

Rahul smiled gently.

— "And the biggest lesson."

Just then, a small voice called out from behind them,

— "Big brothers!"

The four of them turned around.

Simpa was running toward them.

She ran up, took Pradip's hand, and said,

— "You didn't get angry today, did you?"

Pradip smiled and said,

— "No."

Simpa said,

— "Then you're a good boy now!"

All four of them burst out laughing.

Ashok stood a little way off, watching the scene.

It struck him—

sometimes a small child's innocent smile can do something even the wisest lecture never could.

Final Words

The four friends never forgot their past.

Instead, they carried that mistake with them as the greatest lesson of their lives.

They had come to understand—

Need can test a person, but it never makes a wrong act right.

Insult can break a person, but it never grants the right to choose the path of wrongdoing.

And, just as much, they had also learned—

One wrong decision can end a person's entire life, but genuine remorse, courage, and honest hard work can build that life anew.

Simpa's little smile had brought four young men back from the edge of crime's darkness.

And from that day on, a new chapter of their lives had begun—

Not of crime, but of honesty.
Not of despair, but of hard work.
Not of defeat, but of starting over.
